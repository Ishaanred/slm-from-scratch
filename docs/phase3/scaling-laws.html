Context

What changed from Phase 2

Phase 1 was "does the architecture work." Phase 2 was "is the data any good." Phase 3 is the first phase that's actually experimental — you're not building one thing, you're running the same thing many times under different conditions and comparing the outcomes.

Phase 2

  • One training run to confirm the pipeline works
  • Success = "does it run without breaking"
Phase 3

Same harness, many conditions

  • Same architecture, 3 sizes × up to 4 token counts
  • 10 separate training runs, hours each
  • Success = "what does the pattern across runs say"
Concept

What is a scaling law

A scaling law is just an observed, fairly predictable relationship: as you increase model size and training data together, loss falls in a smooth, roughly power-law curve. Not a hard physical law — an empirical pattern that's held up remarkably well across many labs, architectures, and datasets since it was first published.

Analogy: Baking a cake at different oven sizes. A bigger oven (bigger model) can produce more per batch, but only if you also bring proportionally more batter (more data). A huge oven with a thimble of batter wastes most of its capacity. A tiny oven with a truck of batter can't use most of what you brought. Scaling laws are the recipe ratio that keeps oven size and batter amount in balance.

Why this matters practically: without a scaling law, "how big should my model be" is a guess. With one, it's a calculation — given a compute budget, there's a predictable best split between parameters and tokens, and deviating from it in either direction wastes compute.

Concept

FLOPs, in plain terms

FLOPs (floating-point operations) are just the unit for "how much raw compute did this run cost," independent of what GPU you happened to run it on. It's what lets you compare a run on your 5070 Ti to a run OpenAI did on a cluster — same unit, different hardware.

For a transformer, compute cost scales with both model size and how much data you push through it: double the parameters or double the tokens, and you've roughly doubled the FLOPs spent. This is why "loss vs FLOPs" is the x-axis scaling law plots use instead of "loss vs training time" — time depends on your specific GPU, FLOPs doesn't.

A commonly used approximation for a transformer's training compute is C ≈ 6 · N · D, where N is the parameter count and D is the number of training tokens. The 6 accounts for one forward pass (≈2 FLOPs per parameter per token) plus backward pass (≈4 FLOPs per parameter per token, roughly double the forward cost). It's an approximation — attention has its own cost that scales with sequence length separately, but for typical context lengths relative to model size, the 6ND term dominates.
Concept

The Chinchilla rule

In 2022, DeepMind ran a large sweep of models at different size/data combinations (the "Chinchilla" paper) and found that most large models up to that point were oversized relative to how much data they'd been trained on. Their headline result: for a fixed compute budget, the best split is roughly 20 tokens of training data per parameter.

Analogy: A student who spent all semester picking an expensive textbook (bigger model) but barely opened it (little data) does worse on the exam than a student with a cheaper textbook (smaller model) who actually read it cover to cover several times (more data), for the same total study hours (same compute).

Why this project cares

The whole point of Phase 3's grid is to check this ratio against your own architecture and your own data, not just take DeepMind's number on faith. Some cells in the grid below sit well under the 20x ratio on purpose — that's what "undertrained" looks like on a plot, and part of the point is seeing it happen.

Concept

Undertrained vs overtrained

Undertrained means: relative to its parameter count, the model hasn't seen enough tokens to reach the loss its size is capable of. More parameters without more data mostly buys unused capacity. This is what happened with Phase 1's 77M vs 50M comparison — same ~80M tokens, and the bigger model came out worse, because 80M tokens wasn't enough for either, and especially not for the bigger one.

Overtrained (relative to Chinchilla-optimal) means: the model has seen more tokens per parameter than the 20x ratio suggests is compute-optimal. It still improves, just with diminishing returns per token — you're paying more training compute than strictly necessary for the loss you get.

Overtraining isn't automatically bad

Chinchilla-optimal minimizes training compute for a target loss. It says nothing about inference cost. A smaller, overtrained model is cheaper to run for the rest of its life than a larger, compute-optimal one at the same loss — which is exactly why many production models (Llama included) are deliberately trained well past the 20x ratio. See the Q&A below.

The experiment

The 3 training runs

Same architecture as Phases 1 and 2 (the hand-written transformer). Started as a 10-run grid across 3 sizes, trimmed to 6, trimmed again to 3 — see the card below for why a bigger grid wasn't actually buying more here. Three runs, two comparisons.

Run Model Tokens Tokens/param What it answers
1 75M 500M ~7.2x Undertrained end of the 75M curve
2 75M 2B ~28.8x Near/above Chinchilla-optimal end of the 75M curve
3 150M 500M ~3.7x Cross-size comparison at matched, scarce data

Run 1 vs Run 2: same model, more data — the within-size trend, whether loss keeps improving as the 75M model sees more tokens. Run 1 vs Run 3: same data, more parameters — the direct callback to Phase 1's own finding that a bigger model can lose to a smaller one when data is scarce, now checked at a different scale.

Why not a bigger grid

The training harness reuses one fixed learning rate and config across every model size instead of retuning per size, which is what real scaling-law studies (including Chinchilla's own) actually do. Combined with a training corpus that's still relatively modest, a 6-10 run grid was never going to produce a textbook-clean curve here — it would mostly add noise-prone data points at real electricity cost (the original 10-run design was an estimated 80-120 GPU hours, ~22-34 kWh). Three runs answering two specific, well-defined questions carries the same practical value (informing Phase 4's model size choice) and the same portfolio value (showing you understand FLOPs and compute tradeoffs, and can reason about a confound) at roughly a tenth of the compute.

Before the grid

Carried over from Phase 2

Phase 2's actual training run used a small ~214M-token filtered sample, sized for training the tokenizer rather than the language model. Phase 3's runs need real token counts up to 2B, so the full OpenWebText corpus (~8.5B tokens) needs to be re-tokenized with the 32K BPE tokenizer before any of the grid can run.

Bonus: doing this also finally gives a clean, corpus-matched comparison between Phase 1's tokenizer (GPT-2) and Phase 2's (your own 32K BPE) — something Phase 2 couldn't produce on its own because its training corpus was ~40x smaller than Phase 1's. See Phase 2 results for why that comparison was inconclusive.

Q&A

Questions

Why not just train the biggest model your GPU can fit?

Because compute is the actual constraint, not VRAM. Your 5070 Ti can probably fit a 300M model in memory. But fitting isn't the same as training it well — if you only have enough compute budget for, say, 500M tokens, a 300M model at that token count is badly undertrained (ratio ~1.7x, far under the ~20x rule), while a 75M model at the same compute budget could see far more tokens per parameter and actually converge properly.

This is the entire point of the grid: "biggest model that fits" and "best model for your compute budget" are frequently different answers, and the only way to know which regime you're in is to actually plot loss against FLOPs, not just parameter count.

If Chinchilla says the optimal ratio is 20x, isn't training past that wasteful?

Wasteful for training compute, not necessarily overall. Chinchilla's result answers one specific question: for a fixed training compute budget, what size/data split gets you the lowest loss. It says nothing about what happens after training — how much it costs to actually run the model for the next year serving requests.

A smaller model trained on far more than its "optimal" 20x tokens (Llama 2 and Llama 3 both do this deliberately) spends more compute in training than Chinchilla-optimal would, in exchange for a permanently cheaper, faster model to deploy. For a company serving billions of requests, that trade is usually worth it. For a from-scratch learning experiment where you're not deploying anything, Chinchilla-optimal is the more informative number to check your own results against — but it's worth knowing real production labs routinely train past it on purpose, for a reason that has nothing to do with the training run itself.
